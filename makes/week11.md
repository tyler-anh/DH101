## Research Notes
I used six sources to investigate the labor behind AI data labeling. Billy Perrigo’s TIME investigation documents the 2021-2022 OpenAI-Sama project in Kenya, including the work, reported pay, worker testimony, and responses from Sama and OpenAI. Muldoon, Cant, Graham, and Ustek Spilda’s academic study examines Sama’s wider data-annotation workforce in Kenya and Uganda; it helps explain outsourcing, insecure work, and pressure to prioritize margins. The Fairwork report evaluates Sama against five fair-work principles. Partnership on AI’s data-enrichment guidelines provide practical standards for pay, task design, and worker communication. The ILO report gives broader context about digital labor in Kenya. Hicks’s article provides the course framework for examining how technical systems encode assumptions and distribute power.

The hardest information to establish was how responsibility and money move through the outsourcing relationship. TIME reports a $12.50 hourly contract rate paid to Sama and lower worker take-home estimates, but these are different measures; the gap is not Sama’s profit. Workers and Sama also gave conflicting accounts of workload, pay, and access to counseling. OpenAI said it did not set productivity targets and understood that opt-out and counseling protections were available, while placing responsibility for pay and mental-health provisions with Sama. Muldoon and colleagues studied Sama’s wider workforce, not just the OpenAI project. The sources do not disclose the exact profit from this contract or every detail of the labor arrangement.

A finding that stands out in the evidence is that annotators were not simply clicking labels. They interpreted harmful and sometimes traumatic text, while their judgments helped make content legible to an AI system. This complicates the idea that AI safety is purely automated. Hicks’s analysis also helped me see that classification systems are built around choices and assumptions, rather than neutral categories; these choices can shape workers’ experiences and opportunities over time.

## Design Statement
I chose an eight-page digital zine because hidden labor behind AI cannot be explained through a single statistic. The format moves readers from the familiar image of an automated chatbot to the people, contracts, and decisions that help make its safety features possible. It connects worker accounts with the economics of outsourcing and Mar Hicks’s analysis of how systems encode assumptions.

The intended audience is students and everyday AI users who may benefit from AI products without knowing who performs the work behind them. I want readers to question the idea that AI safety is produced by technology alone; the Kenyan workers in this case were employed by Sama, not OpenAI.

The visual style draws on investigative and social-justice zines: warm paper tones, charcoal, orange accents, large headings, short sections, and clear evidence labels. The cover uses an AI-generated conceptual collage of anonymous workers, annotation cards, and a computer screen. It is not a photograph and does not depict actual workers. Diagrams show the outsourcing relationship; paired figures distinguish the client-to-vendor rate from reported worker take-home pay; source notes explain where accounts conflict.

I want readers to feel curiosity, then recognize the human judgment and working conditions hidden by a polished AI interface. The final pages raise practical questions about fair pay, safe conditions, transparent contracts, worker voice, and corporate accountability. The aim is not to reject AI, but to ask what responsible AI should require of the people and companies that build it across the wider AI supply chain.
